# Month 1 — Human-Centred Design for AI Products

**Status:** Working course plan  
**Academic reference:** Carnegie Mellon University Human-Computer Interaction Institute (HCII)  
**Level:** Beginner; no programming required for this first month  
**Suggested time:** 4–6 hours per week; sessions can be split or slowed down  
**Important:** This is our own beginner plan informed by CMU's public course descriptions, not an official CMU syllabus.

## Why this course

CMU HCII's published course descriptions include Design of AI Products and Services and Human AI Interaction. They explore AI as a design material, how AI capabilities can support useful products, and issues including agency, initiative, ethics, bias, transparency, confidence, errors, trust, explainability, and mixed-initiative systems.

Official references:
- Design of AI Products and Services: https://www.hcii.cmu.edu/course/design-ai-products-and-services
- Human AI Interaction: https://hcii.cmu.edu/course/human-ai-interaction
- HCII course listings and availability note: https://hcii.cmu.edu/academics/courses

The CMU courses may expect more experience or include programming. This first month is a non-programming foundation that prepares us to reason about AI-powered experiences before deciding whether technical work should be added.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the month, the learner should be able to:
1. Explain the difference between UX, UI, interaction design, and human–AI interaction.
2. Identify a real user goal and a specific problem without jumping too quickly to a solution.
3. Describe what an AI feature can and cannot reliably do, and identify uncertainty and failure cases.
4. Sketch a human–AI workflow that gives the user appropriate control and recovery options.
5. Create a low-fidelity prototype of an AI-assisted experience.
6. Plan and conduct a small, respectful usability test, then improve the design from observations.
7. Present a short case study showing the problem, evidence, design decisions, testing, and limitations.

## The project that runs through the month

Choose one small, everyday task where AI might help. Keep it low-risk: for example, organising learning materials, helping someone understand a complex document, or supporting a person to plan a routine. Avoid health, legal, financial, or other high-stakes decisions for this beginner project.

We will not assume AI is the right answer. The first design question is whether AI adds meaningful value compared with a simpler non-AI feature.

## Week 1 — Understand people, products, and AI

**Goal:** Learn to see an experience as a person trying to achieve a goal, and understand why AI changes the design problem.

### Session 1 — UX, UI, and human–AI interaction
- Learn: UX vs UI vs interaction design; user goals, context, tasks, and outcomes.
- Think first: Describe an app that is easy to look at but difficult to use. What makes it difficult?
- Practice: Pick one familiar app and describe a user goal in one sentence.
- Evidence of learning: Explain the difference between appearance and the full experience in your own words.

### Session 2 — What makes a product problem worth solving?
- Learn: Problem statements, user needs, assumptions, and evidence.
- Think first: Who has the problem, what are they trying to do, and what currently gets in their way?
- Practice: Write a short problem statement without proposing a feature yet.
- Evidence of learning: The statement describes a person, goal, context, and difficulty—not just “we need an AI app.”

### Session 3 — What AI is good at, and where it can fail
- Learn: AI outputs can be useful but uncertain; errors, bias, incomplete context, and overconfidence matter.
- Think first: What would happen if the AI gave a plausible but wrong answer in your chosen task?
- Practice: Make a capability / limitation / failure-case table for the proposed AI feature.
- Evidence of learning: Name at least two failure cases and what a user should be able to do about them.

### Session 4 — Is AI actually needed?
- Learn: AI as one design material among many; compare an AI approach with a simpler rule, search, form, checklist, or human process.
- Think first: What is the simplest design that could solve this problem?
- Practice: Compare an AI and non-AI concept against usefulness, reliability, user effort, and recovery.
- Evidence of learning: Explain why AI is justified—or decide not to use it.

### Session 5 — Week 1 review and design brief
- Recall quiz: UX vs UI; user goal; one AI strength; two limitations; when not to use AI.
- Project: Produce a one-page brief describing the user, task, problem, why AI may or may not help, and key risks.
- Feedback: Identify one strong point, one assumption to check, and one next step.

**Week 1 deliverable:** One-page problem and AI-fit brief.

## Week 2 — Research users and frame the right problem

**Goal:** Use evidence rather than guesswork to understand what people need.

### Session 6 — Ask useful research questions
- Learn: Interviews, observation, task walkthroughs, desk research, and the difference between a question and a leading question.
- Think first: What would you need to learn before designing anything?
- Practice: Draft five open questions that do not suggest the answer.
- Evidence of learning: Questions invite examples of real behaviour rather than only opinions.

### Session 7 — Observe a task
- Learn: Watch what happens, record exact observations, and separate evidence from interpretation.
- Practice: Walk through a familiar task yourself or observe a willing participant with consent. If no participant is available, label the exercise as a self-observation, not user research.
- Evidence of learning: A table with observation, possible interpretation, and what remains unknown.

### Session 8 — Turn findings into user needs
- Learn: Patterns, user needs, constraints, and avoiding overgeneralisation from small samples.
- Practice: Summarise up to three needs and cite the observation behind each.
- Evidence of learning: Every claimed need has evidence or is explicitly labelled as a hypothesis.

### Session 9 — Trust, control, and consent
- Learn: What the user should know about AI involvement; permission, data minimisation, explainability, control, correction, and opting out.
- Think first: What should the user be able to check, change, undo, or refuse?
- Practice: Add user-control requirements to the brief.
- Evidence of learning: The design does not force the user to accept an AI output as final.

### Session 10 — Week 2 review and research summary
- Recall quiz: leading questions, observation vs interpretation, hypothesis vs evidence, consent, and control.
- Project: Create a concise research summary and update the design brief.
- Feedback: Which conclusion is best supported? Which needs more evidence?

**Week 2 deliverable:** A small research summary and revised requirements.

## Week 3 — Design the human–AI interaction

**Goal:** Plan the interaction, including uncertainty and recovery—not just the ideal path.

### Session 11 — Map the task flow
- Learn: Start state, user action, AI action, response, user decision, and end state.
- Practice: Draw the happy path and label what the user does versus what the AI does.
- Evidence of learning: The user remains able to understand what is happening and make meaningful choices.

### Session 12 — Design for uncertainty and errors
- Learn: Communicating confidence carefully, asking for clarification, showing sources or reasons when appropriate, handling missing information, and recovery.
- Think first: What should happen when the AI is unsure or misunderstands?
- Practice: Add at least three non-happy paths to the flow.
- Evidence of learning: Each failure state gives the user a sensible next action.

### Session 13 — Sketch screens and states
- Learn: Low-fidelity wireframes, content hierarchy, labels, status, empty states, loading, errors, and accessibility basics.
- Practice: Sketch 3–5 screens or states on paper or with a simple tool.
- Evidence of learning: Someone else can follow the basic task without a detailed verbal explanation.

### Session 14 — Decide who takes initiative
- Learn: When the AI should suggest, ask, act, wait, or stop; how to avoid surprising the user.
- Think first: Which actions should require confirmation? Which could be safely automatic?
- Practice: Mark each action in the flow as user-led, AI-suggested, or automatic, and explain why.
- Evidence of learning: Initiative and permissions match the impact and reversibility of the action.

### Session 15 — Week 3 critique
- Recall quiz: task flow, error state, user control, AI initiative, accessibility.
- Project: Walk through the wireframes as if you were a new user; revise the most confusing part.
- Feedback: Give one piece of evidence-based critique and one alternative to consider.

**Week 3 deliverable:** Task flow and low-fidelity prototype, including error and recovery states.

## Week 4 — Test, improve, and communicate the design

**Goal:** Evaluate the design with realistic tasks and use findings to make improvements.

### Session 16 — Write a usability test
- Learn: Task scenarios, neutral prompts, observing behaviour, and avoiding leading participants.
- Practice: Write three tasks a participant could attempt without being told exactly how to use the design.
- Evidence of learning: Tasks reflect real goals, not instructions that give away the answer.

### Session 17 — Conduct a small test
- Practice: If possible, test with 1–3 willing people and obtain consent. Otherwise, conduct a clearly labelled walkthrough or cognitive rehearsal; do not call it empirical user testing.
- Record: Where the person hesitated, made an error, asked a question, or misunderstood the AI.
- Evidence of learning: Notes distinguish observed behaviour from the tester's interpretation.

### Session 18 — Prioritise findings
- Learn: Severity, frequency, user impact, confidence in the finding, and limits of a small sample.
- Practice: Choose the two most important issues and explain why they matter.
- Evidence of learning: Priorities are based on user impact, not personal preference alone.

### Session 19 — Iterate and retest
- Practice: Revise the prototype, explain which evidence prompted each change, and walk through the tasks again.
- Evidence of learning: At least one change is clearly linked to a finding; unresolved issues remain visible.

### Session 20 — Final review and reflection
- Present: The problem, research evidence, why AI is or is not appropriate, the interaction flow, prototype, test findings, and revisions.
- Reflection: What changed in your thinking? What remains uncertain? What would you investigate next?
- Final check: Explain one design decision without relying on “it looks better.”

**Week 4 deliverable:** A mini case study with prototype, findings, revisions, and limitations.

## Final assessment rubric

Use this to guide improvement, not to label the learner.

| Criterion | Evidence to look for |
|---|---|
| Problem framing | Specific user, goal, context, and problem |
| Evidence | Clear separation between observations, assumptions, and conclusions |
| AI judgement | Sensible decision about whether AI adds value; realistic limits and failure cases |
| Human agency | User understands the AI's role and can check, correct, refuse, or recover |
| Interaction design | Coherent flow and useful error states |
| Evaluation | Neutral tasks, honest findings, and recognition of sample limitations |
| Iteration | Design changes trace back to evidence |
| Communication | Clear explanation of decisions, uncertainty, and next steps |

## Weekly rhythm

A flexible 4–6 hours per week:
- 2 short learning sessions (about 30–45 minutes each)
- 1 practical design session (about 60–90 minutes)
- 1 review / feedback session (about 30–45 minutes)
- Remaining time for reading, observing examples, and improving the project

If a concept is not understood, pause and teach it another way rather than pushing through to stay on schedule.

## Course integrity and boundaries

- This plan is an original synthesis based on public CMU course descriptions. It is not a copy of the university's restricted syllabus or a substitute for formal teaching.
- Some CMU course versions include programming, weekly projects, and readings. This beginner month deliberately focuses on design reasoning and does not claim to reproduce the technical course.
- Use low-risk example tasks. Do not test with sensitive personal data or make high-stakes AI decisions.
- Keep all lesson notes, assignments, drafts, feedback, and progress records under the schoolwork folder.
