## Why study the history?
History helps us notice that interface design changes when computers, users, and tasks change. The key question is: **Who has to adapt to whom—the person to the computer, or the computer to the person's goals?**

## A simple timeline of changing interaction

### 1. Computers operated by specialists
Early computers were costly, limited, and often accessed through specialists or prepared batches of instructions. Most people did not sit at a personal screen and interact continuously. The system's capabilities and operating process shaped what the person could do.

### 2. Interactive command-based computing
When people could interact directly through terminals, they could issue commands and receive responses. This made computing more immediate, but users often had to learn the system's vocabulary and remember exact commands.

🟨 **Command interface:** The user types instructions in a system-specific language.  
**Design lesson:** Clear feedback matters; errors can be hard to recover from if the system gives cryptic messages.

### 3. Graphical interfaces and personal computing
Screens, menus, icons, windows, and pointing devices made many actions visible. People could select objects and see results without memorising every command. The interface began to take on more responsibility for showing choices and responding clearly.

🟨 **Direct manipulation:** The person acts on visible objects and sees the effect of the action.  
**Design lesson:** A visible file and a clear rename action can be easier to understand than an unexplained command.

### 4. Web, mobile, and connected systems
People began using digital services across devices, places, and social settings. HCI had to consider more than the screen: context, access, privacy, collaboration, and how systems behave across a whole journey.

**Design lesson:** The same task may begin on a phone and continue on a computer; the system needs to preserve the person's context.

### 5. Machine learning and adaptive interfaces
Systems began using data to classify, rank, recommend, or recognise patterns. Some behaviour was no longer a fixed response to a single button press. The system might infer what the person wants, and could be wrong.

🟨 **Adaptive system:** A system whose output or behaviour changes in response to data, context, or inferred needs.  
**Design lesson:** Make important inferences visible, allow correction, and avoid treating a prediction as a confirmed fact.

### 6. Generative AI and agent-like tools
People can describe a goal in everyday language and receive new text, images, code, or plans. Some tools can take multiple steps or use other tools. This creates new design questions: What did the AI infer? What did it actually do? Can the person inspect, guide, stop, or undo its actions?

🟨 **Mixed-initiative interaction:** A person and system can each take the lead at different moments, while coordinating their actions.  
**Design lesson:** AI may propose a file rename, but the person should understand the proposal and retain the agreed decision.

These stages are a teaching map, not a claim that every technology followed one path or that newer systems make older interfaces disappear.

## Connect the history to Sadhana
Consider naming your paper “Sadhana.”

- A command interface might require you to type an exact rename command.
- A graphical interface might show the file, a rename option, and the new name before you confirm.
- A generative AI tool might suggest alternative titles after reading your paper.
- An agent might offer to rename the file and update references.

As the system takes more initiative, the person needs clear choices and reliable information about what it changed. The fact that a system can act does not mean it has permission to act.

2. A graphical interface.
3. A generative AI or agent interface.

For each, say what the person must understand, what the system should show, and which decision stays with the person.

## Start with the key words

Before the history, define the terms used in the title:

- 🟨 **Artificial intelligence (AI):** computer systems designed to do tasks that people associate with intelligence, such as recognising speech or generating text. This broad term covers different methods.
- 🟨 **Human–computer interaction (HCI):** the study and design of how people use computer systems. For example, it asks whether a file-renaming screen makes the choices clear and helps people recover from mistakes.
- 🟨 **Rule-based program:** software that follows instructions a programmer wrote explicitly. For example: “If the filename contains ‘invoice’, suggest the Finance folder.” “Hand-written rules” means people wrote the instructions into the program; it does not mean the AI itself was written on paper.
- 🟨 **Machine learning (ML):** a way of building some AI systems by training them on examples so they can find patterns, instead of writing every rule by hand. For example, show a system many files labelled “Finance” or “Sadhana,” then check whether it can suggest a folder for a new file.
- 🟨 **Training:** using examples to adjust a model so it learns patterns. In everyday AI products, asking one question does not necessarily retrain the model; it usually means the system is using the current conversation as input. Do not assume that it permanently learned from one correction.

Now the history makes more sense: early AI often used programmer-written rules; later machine-learning methods learned patterns from examples. HCI studies how people interact with those systems and how to make that interaction useful and understandable.
