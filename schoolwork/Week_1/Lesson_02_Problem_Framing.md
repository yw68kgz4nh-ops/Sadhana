# Lesson 2 — Define the Problem Before Designing the Solution

**Estimated time:** 30–45 minutes  
**Lesson status:** 🔴 Not yet taught or assessed  
**Main question:** How do we describe a real problem without jumping straight to a feature?

## 1. Learning outcomes

By the end, you should be able to:
- identify a person, goal, context, and difficulty;
- separate an observed fact from an assumption;
- write a problem statement that does not prescribe the solution;
- identify what you still need to find out.

## 2. Start with a weak brief

Consider this statement:

> We need an AI assistant with a dashboard and automatic project folders.

What do we know about the actual user problem? Very little. We do not yet know who needs it, what they are trying to do, what goes wrong, or whether AI and automatic folders would help.

A proposed feature is not the same thing as a well-understood problem.

## 3. The four-part problem frame

A useful first frame contains:

1. **Person:** Who is experiencing the difficulty?
2. **Goal:** What are they trying to achieve?
3. **Context:** When, where, and under what conditions does the task happen?
4. **Difficulty and impact:** What gets in the way, and why does it matter?

Then add a fifth item:

5. **Evidence or uncertainty:** What did we actually observe, and what are we only guessing?

### Example

**Too vague:** “People need better AI project management.”

**More useful:** “A person continuing a multi-session design project may struggle to identify the latest confirmed document and next task when project notes are spread across conversations and files. This can lead to repeated searching and uncertainty about whether work was saved. The frequency and severity of the problem still need to be measured.”

The second statement describes a situation and impact without assuming the answer must be a dashboard, a folder system, or an AI feature. It also makes the remaining uncertainty visible.

## 4. Evidence, interpretation, and proposal are different

Use three separate labels:

- **Observed:** What was directly seen, heard, or recorded.
- **Interpretation / hypothesis:** What we think may explain the observation.
- **Proposal:** A change we might test.

Example:

- **Observed:** A user opened a conversation and could not immediately locate the document they expected.
- **Hypothesis:** The relationship between project, conversation, and file location may not be clear enough.
- **Proposal:** Show a persistent project breadcrumb and a direct link to the active document.

The proposal may be good, but it is not proven by the observation alone. Other explanations may exist. We should test the proposal rather than treat our first idea as a fact.

## 5. Mini worksheet

Complete the following in plain language:

- **Person:** …
- **Goal:** …
- **Context:** …
- **Current difficulty:** …
- **Impact on the person:** …
- **What I directly observed:** …
- **What I am assuming:** …
- **What I still need to learn:** …

If you cannot answer one of the questions, write “unknown.” An honest gap is more useful than an invented detail.

## 6. Mini example — improve the statement

**Initial statement:** “The app should automatically summarise every file.”

Questions to ask:
- Who needs a summary?
- What task are they trying to complete?
- What is difficult about their current approach?
- Do they need a summary, a search function, a clearer index, or something else?
- What could go wrong if the summary omits a key detail?
- Would automatic summarising create unwanted work or privacy risks?

**Improved problem frame:** “A learner reviewing several long course documents may find it difficult to locate the concepts needed for a particular assignment. We have not yet established whether summarising, search, topic labels, or another approach would reduce that difficulty. We should first observe how the learner currently finds relevant information.”

The improved frame creates room for several possible solutions.

## 7. A problem statement pattern

Use this as a starting point, not a rigid formula:

> [Person] is trying to [goal] in [context], but [specific difficulty] makes it harder to [impact]. We know this because [evidence]. We still need to learn [uncertainty].

If there is no direct evidence yet, say so:

> We suspect that …, but this is currently a hypothesis that needs checking.

## 8. Practical task — frame one real problem

Choose a small, familiar task. Do not select a high-stakes decision.

Write:
1. One sentence describing the person and their goal.
2. One sentence describing the difficulty and impact.
3. One fact you know directly.
4. One assumption you need to test.
5. One question you would investigate before designing.

Do not name the solution in the problem statement. You may list possible solutions separately after the problem is clear.

## 9. Exit check

1. Why is “we need an AI app” not a strong problem statement?
2. What is the difference between an observation and a hypothesis?
3. What should you write when important evidence is missing?
4. Why can jumping to a solution too early waste design effort?

### Tutor notes — check after attempting

1. It describes a proposed technology, not the person's goal, context, or difficulty.
2. An observation records what happened; a hypothesis is a possible explanation that still needs testing.
3. Mark the detail as unknown and identify how it could be investigated.
4. The proposed feature may solve the wrong problem or introduce new effort, risk, or complexity.

## 10. Revision card

**Front:** What should a useful early problem statement contain?

**Back:** A person, goal, context, specific difficulty, impact, and evidence or clearly labelled uncertainty—without prematurely prescribing the solution.

## 11. Learning record

Record what was attempted, what was demonstrated, what remains uncertain, and the next step. Do not treat a drafted problem statement as validated user research.
