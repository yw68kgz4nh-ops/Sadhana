# Week 1 Revision Cards

Use these for short recall practice. Try to answer the **Front** before opening the **Back**. It is fine to answer aloud.

## Card 1 — UX
**Front:** What does UX describe?

**Back:** The person's overall experience of using or encountering a product or service, including whether it helps them achieve their goal and what effort, confusion, or confidence the experience involves.

## Card 2 — UI
**Front:** What is UI, and why is it not the whole of UX?

**Back:** UI is the visible and operable interface—such as screens, controls, text, and menus. UX is broader and includes the entire experience and whether the person's goal is achieved.

## Card 3 — Interaction design
**Front:** What does interaction design describe?

**Back:** How actions, system responses, choices, feedback, and recovery fit together over time.

## Card 4 — Human–AI interaction
**Front:** What extra questions matter when AI is involved?

**Back:** What the AI can do, what may go wrong, how uncertainty is communicated, when the AI should act or ask, and how the person can inspect, correct, refuse, or recover.

## Card 5 — Problem framing
**Front:** What should an early problem frame identify?

**Back:** The person, goal, context, specific difficulty and impact, plus evidence or explicitly labelled uncertainty.

## Card 6 — Observation versus hypothesis
**Front:** How do an observation and a hypothesis differ?

**Back:** An observation records what was directly seen, heard, or recorded. A hypothesis is a possible explanation that still needs checking.

## Card 7 — Feature versus problem
**Front:** Why is “we need an AI dashboard” not a problem statement?

**Back:** It prescribes a solution without explaining the person's goal, context, difficulty, impact, or supporting evidence.

## Card 8 — Capability
**Front:** What is an AI capability?

**Back:** Something the system can do under specified conditions, such as generating a draft summary from supplied text.

## Card 9 — Limitation versus failure
**Front:** How is a limitation different from a failure case?

**Back:** A limitation constrains performance; a failure case describes a specific unacceptable outcome in a task or workflow.

## Card 10 — Recovery
**Front:** What should be designed alongside an AI feature?

**Back:** Ways for the person to detect a problem, check the source, correct or undo an action where possible, retry, or use a fallback.

## Card 11 — AI fit
**Front:** What question should come before “How can we add AI?”

**Back:** “What is the person's real problem, and would AI solve it better than a simpler alternative?”

## Card 12 — Honest status
**Front:** Why must a design distinguish a draft from a verified result?

**Back:** Because a plausible-looking output is not proof of correctness or successful saving. People need an accurate account of what has and has not been checked.

## Card 13 — Evidence
**Front:** What should you do when evidence is missing?

**Back:** State what is unknown, label assumptions as hypotheses, and identify a practical way to investigate them.

## Card 14 — Provisional decision
**Front:** Is “we need more evidence” an acceptable design conclusion?

**Back:** Yes. It is often the most responsible conclusion when the evidence is not strong enough to justify a choice.

## Quick mixed review

Answer without looking at the cards:
1. Give an example of a good UI with a poor UX.
2. Write a problem statement without naming a solution.
3. Describe one AI failure, its impact, and a recovery.
4. Compare one AI and one non-AI approach.
5. Name one piece of evidence that could change your design decision.

Revisit any card that is difficult. A wrong answer is useful information about what to practise next, not a judgement about ability.
