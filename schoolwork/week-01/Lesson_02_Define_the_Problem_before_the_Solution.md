# Week 1 · Lesson 2 — Define the Problem Before the Solution

**Course:** Human-Centred Design for AI Products  
**Level:** Beginner · No programming required  
**Suggested time:** 45–60 minutes  
**Status:** Prepared study paper; complete only after we work through it together.

## Learning goals

By the end, you should be able to:
- distinguish a user problem from a proposed solution;
- write a focused problem statement;
- separate observations, assumptions, and questions that need evidence.

## 1. Think first

Suppose someone says: “We need an AI assistant for students.”

Before agreeing, ask yourself:
- Which students?
- What are they trying to do?
- What is difficult about it now?
- What makes us think this is a real problem?
- Could a simpler solution help?

Write down your first reaction. We will revisit it at the end.

## 2. Problem versus solution

A **problem** describes a difficulty a person experiences while pursuing a goal. A **solution** is one possible response to that difficulty.

For example:
- **Solution-first statement:** “We need a chatbot to organise files.”
- **Problem statement:** “A learner preparing for a lesson needs to find the latest version of their notes, but the files are spread across several folders and have inconsistent names.”

The second statement gives us something to investigate. Maybe the learner needs better search, clearer naming, a simple index, or a chatbot. We should not decide before understanding the problem.

## 3. The four parts of a useful problem statement

1. **Person:** Who experiences the difficulty?
2. **Goal:** What are they trying to accomplish?
3. **Context:** When or under what conditions does it happen?
4. **Barrier:** What makes the goal difficult?

Optional fifth part: **Evidence or uncertainty.** What have we observed, and what are we only guessing?

A good statement is focused enough to guide research but not so narrow that it secretly dictates the answer.

## 4. Facts, assumptions, and questions

Designers often have incomplete information. Make that visible.

- **Observation:** Something directly seen or reliably reported. Example: “I needed three searches to find the document.”
- **Assumption:** Something we believe but have not checked. Example: “Other learners probably have the same problem.”
- **Question:** Something we need to investigate. Example: “Would a single index make the document easier to find?”

Do not present assumptions as established facts. One person's experience can be a valuable starting point, but it does not automatically represent everyone.

## 5. Worked example

**Initial idea:** “Build an AI planner.”

**Possible user and goal:** An adult learner wants to choose what to study next without losing track of earlier work.

**Possible barrier:** The course materials, unfinished tasks, and next steps are recorded in different places.

**What we know:** Perhaps the learner has reported searching several places before resuming work.

**What we do not know yet:** Whether the main difficulty is finding materials, deciding priorities, remembering progress, or something else.

**Problem statement:** “When returning to a learning project after a break, a learner needs to understand what has been completed and what to do next, but the current records do not make the next action obvious.”

Notice that the statement leaves room for a checklist, a clearer dashboard, a short progress note, or AI assistance.

## 6. Guided practice

Write a problem statement for one everyday task you would genuinely like to improve. Use this template:

> “[Person] wants to [goal] when [context], but [barrier] makes it difficult. I know this because [evidence or observation]. I still need to find out [open question].”

If you do not have evidence yet, say so plainly: “This is an initial hypothesis; I have not tested it.”

Now check your statement:
- Does it describe a person rather than “users” in general?
- Is the goal concrete?
- Is the barrier specific?
- Does it avoid prescribing an AI solution?
- Can you name at least one thing you still need to learn?

## 7. Check your understanding

1. Why is “build a chatbot” a solution rather than a problem statement?
2. What is the difference between an observation and an assumption?
3. Improve this statement: “People need a better app.”
4. Name one piece of evidence that would make your problem statement more convincing.

## 8. Exit ticket

Complete:
- “The problem I want to investigate is …”
- “One assumption I must not treat as fact is …”
- “The next thing I need to learn is …”

## Completion evidence

The lesson is complete when the learner can write a focused problem statement and identify at least one assumption or unanswered question. The statement can change when new evidence appears; revision is part of design.
