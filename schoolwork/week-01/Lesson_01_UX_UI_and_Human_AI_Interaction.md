# Week 1 · Lesson 1 — UX, UI, and Human–AI Interaction

**Course:** Human-Centred Design for AI Products  
**Level:** Beginner; no programming required  
**Suggested time:** 45–60 minutes  
**Status:** Prepared study paper. It is complete only after we work through it together.

## Learning goals

By the end, you should be able to explain UX, UI, and interaction design; describe an experience from the user's point of view; and identify a user goal.

## 1. Think first

Choose an app or website you know. Have you used something that looked attractive but was frustrating? What were you trying to do, and what made it difficult? Write a few sentences before reading on. You do not need to know the terminology yet.

## 2. The main ideas

**User experience (UX)** is the overall experience a person has while trying to achieve something with a product or service. It includes whether the product is understandable, useful, accessible, reliable, and reassuring—not just whether it looks good.

**User interface (UI)** is the part a person directly sees or operates: screens, text, buttons, menus, controls, and visual feedback.

**Interaction design** concerns how the product behaves as a person uses it: what happens after a click, what choices are offered, what feedback appears, and how a person recovers from a mistake.

**Human–AI interaction** adds questions about how a person and an AI system share work. Does the AI suggest or decide? Can the person inspect the result? How does the system express uncertainty? What happens when it is wrong?

These ideas overlap but are not interchangeable. A clear-looking button is UI. What happens when it is pressed is interaction design. Whether the whole process helps the person achieve their goal is part of UX.

## 3. Worked example

Imagine a learning app with a button labelled **“Summarise this chapter.”**

- **UI:** The button is readable and easy to find.
- **Interaction design:** The app shows that it is working, presents a summary, and lets the learner return to the chapter.
- **UX:** The summary is understandable, saves time, and helps the learner study without hiding important information.
- **Human–AI interaction:** The summary may omit a key point or make a mistake. The learner needs a way to check the original, request a revision, and remain in control.

A polished interface cannot rescue a workflow that loses the user's work. A technically impressive AI feature is not automatically useful.

## 4. Describe a user goal

Use this sentence:

> **[Person] wants to [goal] in [context], but [difficulty] makes it hard.**

Example: “A student wants to find the main ideas in a long reading before class, but the chapter is difficult to scan quickly.”

This describes a person, goal, context, and difficulty. It does not jump straight to “build an AI summariser.”

## 5. Guided practice

Pick a familiar app or service and complete:

- **Person:** Who is using it?
- **Goal:** What are they trying to achieve?
- **Context:** When or where are they doing it?
- **Task:** What steps do they take?
- **Difficulty:** What gets in the way?
- **Successful outcome:** How would they know they had succeeded?

Then label one detail as UI, one as interaction design, and one as UX.

## 6. Check your understanding

Answer in your own words:

1. What is the difference between UI and UX?
2. Give an example of interaction design.
3. Why does an AI feature need ways to check or correct its output?
4. In “A person wants to organise course notes, but files are scattered across folders,” identify the person, goal, and difficulty.

**Hint:** Start with the person's goal, not the feature you want to build.

## 7. Exit ticket

Finish these sentences:
- “I used to think UX was …; now I think …”
- “One thing I need to learn about the user's goal is …”

## Completion evidence

This lesson is ready to mark complete when you can explain UX, UI, and interaction design in your own words and describe one real user goal. We will record your answer and any questions in our progress notes.
