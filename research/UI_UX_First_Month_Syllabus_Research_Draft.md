# UI/UX Learning Programme: First-Month Syllabus (Research Draft)

**Status:** Research-backed proposal, not an official university syllabus  
**Prepared:** 19 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Build a beginner-friendly first month of UI/UX learning using public university module descriptions and assessment patterns as reference points.

## 1. Research basis

This plan draws on public course/module information from universities. These are reference points, not a claim that this plan reproduces any university's course exactly.

- **University of Leeds — User Experience / Interaction Design module:** UX, interaction design and UI distinctions; understanding users through activities; heuristic evaluation; usability testing; methods and tools; portfolio-building. The module includes design research and basic physical/digital prototyping.  
  https://catalogue.leeds.ac.uk/Module/UG/PDES/3180/202627
- **University of Edinburgh — Fundamentals of User Experience Design (2026/27):** design thinking, UX research, information architecture, sketching and wireframing, prototyping and testing, UI design, UX documentation.  
  https://www.drps.ed.ac.uk/26-27/dpt/cxdesi11198.htm
- **University of Bath — Human-Computer Interaction 1:** interaction design principles, prototypes for defined user groups, UI/UX design, accessibility, and ethical considerations in user testing.  
  https://www.bath.ac.uk/catalogues/2025-2026/CM/CM22011.html
- **University of Kent — Human Computer Interaction:** user-centred interface design, usability principles, interface analysis, design, testing and evaluation.  
  https://www.kent.ac.uk/courses/modules/module/COMP4102
- **Cardiff University — Design Thinking and Prototyping for User Experience:** a staged progression from UX foundations and user research to requirements, ideation, wireframing/prototyping, accessibility, evaluation and portfolio work.  
  https://data.cardiff.ac.uk/legacy/grails/module/CM3116.html

## 2. Learning outcomes for the first month

By the end of four weeks, a beginner should be able to:

1. Explain the difference between user experience (UX), user interface (UI), interaction design and human-computer interaction (HCI).
2. Describe a real user's goal, context and difficulty without immediately jumping to a solution.
3. Carry out a small, ethical piece of user research and record evidence carefully.
4. Identify usability problems using simple heuristics and accessibility-aware thinking.
5. Map a basic task flow and create low-fidelity wireframes or a clickable prototype.
6. Test a prototype with a small number of people, summarise findings, and make evidence-based improvements.
7. Present the process and learning in a short portfolio case study.

## 3. Four-week plan

### Week 1 — What UX/UI is, and how to notice design problems

**Learn**
- UX, UI, interaction design and HCI: how they differ and overlap.
- User goals, tasks, context, feedback, consistency and affordances.
- Usability basics: effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction.
- Observe an interface before judging it; separate observation from assumption.

**Practice**
- Choose a familiar website, app or physical-digital service.
- Complete one common task and record each step, confusion point and expectation.
- Write a one-page heuristic critique with screenshots or sketches where useful.

**Deliverable:** Interface critique with 3–5 specific usability observations and evidence.

### Week 2 — Understanding users and their needs

**Learn**
- User-centred design and the research-to-design cycle.
- Interview questions, observation, short surveys and desk research.
- Avoiding leading questions and distinguishing evidence from guesses.
- Research ethics, consent, privacy and inclusive design/accessibility basics.

**Practice**
- Pick a small, everyday problem and define the audience and research question.
- Draft a short interview/observation plan.
- If appropriate and with consent, speak to 2–3 people; otherwise use a clearly labelled hypothetical exercise and do not present it as real research.
- Summarise patterns and uncertainties without overstating the sample.

**Deliverable:** One-page research plan and a concise evidence-based needs summary.

### Week 3 — Information architecture, flows and wireframes

**Learn**
- Turning user needs into requirements.
- Information architecture, labels and navigation.
- Task flows and common interaction states.
- Sketching and low-fidelity wireframes; why rough prototypes are useful.
- Accessibility considerations such as readable text, clear labels and non-colour-only cues.

**Practice**
- Choose one clearly bounded task from the Week 2 problem.
- Draw its current or proposed task flow.
- Sketch 3–5 key screens/states and make a simple paper or digital prototype.
- Explain which research finding informed each major design choice.

**Deliverable:** Task-flow diagram plus a low-fidelity prototype and short design rationale.

### Week 4 — Usability testing, iteration and portfolio

**Learn**
- Difference between testing a design and testing a person.
- Task-based usability tests and think-aloud prompts.
- Observing task completion, hesitation, errors and questions.
- Prioritising issues, iterating, and acknowledging limitations.

**Practice**
- Prepare 3 realistic tasks for the prototype.
- Test with 2–3 people where possible, with consent; do not collect unnecessary personal data.
- Record observations rather than leading participants.
- Prioritise the most important issues and revise the prototype.
- Write a short reflection on what changed and what remains unknown.

**Deliverable:** Brief usability-test report, revised prototype and a mini case study documenting problem, evidence, design decisions, test findings and iteration.

## 4. Suggested weekly rhythm

A manageable starting rhythm is 4–6 hours per week:
- 1–2 hours: lessons and reading
- 1 hour: examples and critique
- 2 hours: practical assignment
- 30–60 minutes: reflection and portfolio notes

This is a proposed pace, not a university credit-hour equivalence. It can be adjusted to the learner.

## 5. Assessment rubric for the first month

Use this rubric for feedback, not as a formal university grade.

| Criterion | What good beginner work shows |
|---|---|
| Understanding the user/problem | A specific goal and context, with assumptions clearly marked |
| Use of evidence | Observations or research findings support the problem statement and design choices |
| Usability and inclusion | Identifies plausible usability barriers and considers accessibility |
| Design process | Shows flow, sketches/prototype and at least one meaningful iteration |
| Evaluation | Test tasks are clear; findings distinguish observed issues from interpretations |
| Communication/reflection | Explains decisions, limitations and next steps clearly |

## 6. Important boundaries

- This is an original beginner programme informed by public university module descriptions. It is **not** an official syllabus, accredited course, or substitute for university teaching.
- Some source modules are advanced or intended for enrolled students. Their topics and project structures are used as references, while the exercises here are simplified for beginners.
- Research with real people must be voluntary and respectful. For a beginner exercise, keep it low-risk and avoid collecting sensitive personal information.
- Tool choice is secondary to learning the process. Paper sketches are acceptable before moving to a digital design tool.

## 7. Next step

Before expanding into a full lesson pack, confirm the learner's available time, whether they want to focus on digital products (websites/apps) or broader services, and whether they have access to a prototyping tool. The four-week outline above can be used as the working draft in the meantime.
